Chunk 1

There is a particular kind of stubbornness that looks, from the outside, like loss of judgment. It gets up early. It goes outside in the cold. It does things that produce no visible result and then comes back inside and does them again the next day. People who love the person doing this watch with a mixture of concern and frustration, because they cannot see what the person is tending, and the person cannot explain it in a way that satisfies them. This is the story of a man named Otto, a garden in winter, and the people who were certain he had finally gone too far.

Chunk 2

Otto was seventy-one and had gardened the same plot behind his house for thirty-eight years. He knew every corner of it. He knew which parts held water after rain and which dried out first in sun. He knew where the light came in longest and where the frost settled hardest. He had buried two dogs in the far corner, under the old apple tree, and he did not consider this morbid. He considered it correct. The garden had been witness to most of his adult life and he felt, obscurely but firmly, that it deserved his continued attention in return.

Chunk 3

His daughter Nina thought he spent too much time there. She lived forty minutes away and visited on Sundays, and the visits had, over the past year, developed a quality he recognized as management. She asked questions about how he was sleeping. She left food in the refrigerator. She mentioned, twice, a complex of new apartments for people of his age that had a communal garden and a maintenance team and activities on Wednesday afternoons. Otto listened to this carefully and said nothing. He had learned, over seven decades, that saying nothing was occasionally the most efficient response available to him.

Chunk 4

His neighbor Paul was more direct. Paul was fifty-three and had moved in three years ago and communicated largely in practical observations. In November he leaned over the fence and said that Otto's garden looked dead. Otto said it was not dead. Paul said it looked it. Otto said appearance and condition were not the same thing and went inside. He did not find this exchange upsetting. He found it clarifying. There were people who understood that winter was a phase and people who saw bare earth and concluded the enterprise was finished. He had spent his life in the company of both kinds.

Chunk 5

In December Nina came on a Sunday and found him outside in the rain, turning compost into the bed along the south wall. She stood at the kitchen window for a moment before coming out. She did not say what she was thinking. What she said was that she had brought soup. Otto thanked her for the soup. She stood beside him in the rain for a while, watching him work. Then she asked if he could come inside. He said he would come inside when he had finished. She went in. He finished thirty minutes later and came inside and they ate the soup and did not talk about the garden.

Chunk 6

His granddaughter Marta was nine and had no opinion about the garden being dead because she did not think gardens could die, any more than she thought the sky could run out of weather. She came to visit in the school holidays and followed Otto outside without being asked. She did not help, exactly. She walked behind him and picked up things he set down and handed them back when he needed them, which he told her was more useful than it looked. She asked what he was doing. He said he was keeping something company. She considered this and accepted it in the way that nine-year-olds accept things that adults say with sufficient confidence.

Chunk 7

In January the argument happened. Nina arrived on a Sunday to find Otto at the far end of the garden in three degrees of cold, on his knees in the mud beside the apple tree. She came to the back door and called to him twice. He did not hear her. She came out in her coat and stood over him. She said this had to stop. He looked up at her. She said she was serious. She said the neighbors had mentioned it. She said she was worried. She said she needed him to listen to her. Otto got to his feet slowly and looked at her for a moment and then said: I am listening. I just disagree.

Chunk 8

They went inside and the argument continued at the kitchen table. Nina said she was not trying to take anything from him. She said she was trying to make sure he was safe and warm and not alone in a muddy garden in January doing things that could wait until spring. Otto said they could not wait until spring. Nina asked why not. Otto thought about this for longer than Nina found comfortable, and then said: because the garden does not wait until spring, and if I am not paying attention I will miss things that matter. Nina did not know what to say to this. She made tea instead.

Chunk 9

Marta came for a week in February. She was the only one who did not treat the winter garden as a problem to be solved. On the third morning she came outside with Otto and crouched beside the bed along the south wall and looked at it very carefully. She said she could not see anything. Otto said to look again. She looked again. She pointed to something at the soil surface, barely visible. A pale green tip, barely the width of a thread, just emerging. She looked up at Otto. He nodded. She looked back at the green tip and was quiet for a moment. Then she said: that is very small. Otto said: that is how it starts.

Chunk 10

Nina came the following Sunday and Otto showed her the same tip. It was slightly larger. She looked at it for a moment and said it was good. He could hear that she was trying. He appreciated the trying. He did not tell her that the reason he had been in the garden in the rain and the mud and the cold since October was not because he did not know that these things grew on their own. He knew they grew on their own. He went outside because the alternative was sitting inside waiting, and sitting inside waiting was the thing he was no longer willing to do.

Chunk 11

Paul mentioned the garden over the fence in March. He said things were beginning to look more like something. Otto said March had that effect. Paul said he had not really understood, over the winter, what Otto was doing out there. Otto said he was not certain he could have explained it. Paul said his wife had started growing herbs on the kitchen windowsill. Otto said that was a reasonable beginning. Paul seemed to want to say something else and did not. He went inside. Otto stayed where he was, turning the soil at the base of the apple tree where the ground was beginning to soften.

Chunk 12

By April the garden had begun, undeniably, to return. The bed along the south wall held a row of shoots that Otto had been watching since February. The apple tree had its first provisional buds. The compost he had worked in through the winter was visible in the changed quality of the soil, darker and more open. He walked through the garden on a morning in April and felt something he associated with completing a long piece of work, not the completion itself but the moment just before it, when the thing you have been doing begins to confirm itself. He had felt this before, in other winters. It did not get less satisfying.

Chunk 13

Nina came on a Sunday in April and walked through the garden with him for the first time without saying anything about the apartment complex or the maintenance team or activities on Wednesday afternoons. She asked the names of things he showed her. She did not remember the names but she asked them. He told her. They walked the whole length of the garden, from the bed along the south wall to the apple tree at the far end. At the apple tree she stopped and looked at the blossom coming in and said: you knew this was here, all winter. He said he believed it was. She said that was different from knowing. He said he was aware of that.

Chunk 14

Marta came in May and the garden was fully itself again. She ran through it with an energy that Otto watched with something he recognized as pure pleasure. She stopped at the south wall bed and crouched down and found, after some searching, the spot where the green tip had been in February. It was now a substantial plant with several leaves. She looked up at Otto. He nodded. She stood up and continued running. He did not try to explain what it meant to him that she had remembered where to look. He did not try to explain most things to Marta. She usually understood them anyway, in her own terms.

Chunk 15

There are things that do not show their progress. They work in the dark, in the cold, below the surface, without any visible sign that anything is happening. If you judge them by what is visible you will conclude they have failed. If you stop tending them because nothing is visible you will sometimes be right and sometimes be wrong, and you will not know which until it is too late to go back. The harder thing, and Otto knew this, is to continue tending something you cannot yet see. Not because you are certain. Because you have chosen to behave as if it matters, and that choice, made daily in the cold, is the thing that makes it matter. Some winters are very long. Go outside anyway.
